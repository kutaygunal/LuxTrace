LuxTrace Against the Illumination Module

The Keysight modules datasheet declares exactly twenty-one capabilities under "Illumination Module", in two groups. This is a line-by-line audit of all twenty-one — no other module credited on either side.

Why this comparison is much closer than the last one

The whole-product comparison put LuxTrace at roughly a quarter of LightTools. Against the Illumination Module alone it lands at eight of twenty-one fully met and eight more partially met — because this module is the one that measures light, and measuring light is the thing LuxTrace was built to do.

The boundary matters, and it cuts both ways. The datasheet puts geometry, scatter models, volume scattering, coatings, ray splitting and surface patterns in the Core Module, not here. So LuxTrace's BSDF work, its coating solver and its medium stack earn nothing in this report — they compete with Core. Conversely, LightTools' freeform tools, optimizer, GRIN and phosphor are other modules and are not held against LuxTrace here either.

The result in one line

Nineteen of the twenty-one are reachable in roughly six to eight engineer-months, and most of those are weeks rather than months. The remaining two — photorealistic rendering and backward ray tracing — are one shared bet worth nine to eighteen months, and they are the only items here that change what the product is rather than what it covers.

A

Illumination analysis

11 capabilities

Everything downstream of the trace: what gets computed, how it is charted, and what can be asked of the data afterwards. LuxTrace is strong on the numbers and weak on the interrogation — it computes well and filters barely at all.

01

Photorealistic Rendering

A lit-appearance image of the model — what the luminaire looks like switched on. The Core Module renders mechanical models; lit appearance needs this module.

Nothing comparable. The 3D tab is an OCCT shaded viewport with ray legs drawn over it, coloured by energy, bounce count or wavelength — a diagnostic display, not a physically-based render. It also caps at about 6 000 ray legs before a dense bundle draws as a solid tube.

Gap 6–12 mo · needs a camera-side trace first Coupled to item 02. Build backward tracing once and both fall out; build a renderer without it and the image is not photometric.
02

Photometric or radiometric analysis using forward and backward ray tracing

Both unit systems, and both trace directions — source-to-detector and eye-to-source.

Units: met. Source flux in watts or lumens, receiver in W/m² or lux, far field in W/sr or candela, converted through the CIE V(λ) curve, with a test asserting a photometric run delivers the lumens it was asked for. Direction: forward only. Next-event estimation at diffuse bounces is a deterministic connection back to the receiver and the nearest thing in the codebase to a backward step, but there is no camera-originated trace.

Partial 3–6 mo · backward path only The NEE machinery is the head start. Connecting to an arbitrary camera pixel rather than to the receiver is the same estimator with a different endpoint.
03

Illuminance, luminance, luminous intensity

The three photometric quantities: flux per area, flux per area per solid angle, flux per solid angle.

Illuminance and luminous intensity: met. Lux on the receiver grid; candela in the far field, correctly divided by each cell's solid angle so an isotropic source does not read dark at the poles. Luminance: absent. Nothing measures flux per projected area per steradian, which is the quantity glare, display uniformity and lit-appearance specifications are written in.

Partial 1–2 mo · shared with item 20 One implementation covers both. Luminance is what an angular/spatial luminance meter reports; build the meter and the quantity arrives with it.
04

Line charts, raster, contour, and surface charts

Four chart forms over the same receiver data.

Line: met — PlotWidget carries error bars, reference markers and a hover readout across cross-sections, encircled energy, convergence, through-focus and sweeps. Raster: met — HeatmapWidget with five colour maps, linear/log/sqrt scaling, RGB mode and a click-to-move cut line. Contour and 3D surface: absent.

Partial 0.5 mo Marching squares over the existing grid. The data structure and the colour maps are already there; this is chart code, not analysis code.
05

Colorimetric analysis: 1931 and 1976 CIE coordinates, correlated color temperature

Chromaticity in both standard spaces, plus CCT.

CIE 1931 colour-matching functions are implemented in Spectrum and chromaticity and correlated colour temperature are reported in the metrics panel. CIE 1976 u′v′ is a closed-form transform of the same XYZ and is simply not printed — a few lines, not a capability.

Parity <0.1 mo · to add u′v′ Worth doing immediately. u′v′ is the space colour-shift tolerances are actually specified in for LED work.
06

RGB output display, CIE chromaticity chart

A colour rendering of the receiver, and the horseshoe diagram with the result plotted on it.

RGB display: met. Per-band grids and linear sRGB weights per monochromatic stimulus drive an RGB heatmap mode. Chromaticity chart: absent. The coordinates are computed and printed as numbers; there is no diagram to plot them on, and no way to see a colour spread rather than a colour average.

Partial 0.25 mo The spectral locus is already in the codebase as the CIE curves. Drawing the horseshoe and scattering per-bin points on it is a widget, and it is the natural home for a colour-over-angle view later.
07

Post-processing of output data

Deriving new answers from a completed trace without re-tracing it.

Strong, and in one case unusually so. Every receiver arrival is recorded with its incoming direction, so through-focus propagates arrivals analytically to any nearby plane and refines the minimum with a parabolic fit — one trace answers every plane. Encircled energy, cross-sections, spot metrics, MTF and the OPD/wavefront map are all post-processes over the same arrivals. The gap is re-binning: changing receiver resolution still costs a trace.

Parity 0.5 mo · to add re-binning Metrics are already computed as densities so they survive a re-bin unchanged. Storing arrivals rather than only the binned grid is the remaining piece.
08

Receiver data filtering using over a dozen filter types

Ask the same trace many questions: show only rays from this source, in this band, that hit this surface, arrived within this angle, after this many bounces.

One filter exists — the receiver's acceptance half-angle, with a documented choice between absorbing and passing refused flux, booked to its own channel so the loss budget stays honest. Beyond that, nothing. The 3D view's receiver-paths-only toggle is a display filter, not a data one. Per-source arrival tagging already exists and is the one filter that is nearly free today.

Gap 1–1.5 mo The highest value-per-week item in this report. Filtering is what turns one expensive trace into ten answers, and it is the analysis capability illumination engineers reach for constantly.
09

Encircled and ensquared energy

Fraction of flux within a radius, and within a square half-width.

Encircled: met — a full curve plus D50 and D86 radii, validated against a synthetic uniform disc whose encircled-energy radius is known in closed form to be R·√f. Ensquared: absent, and it is the same integration over a different kernel.

Parity <0.1 mo Ensquared matters for sensors and displays, where the pixel is square and the circular figure understates the answer.
10

Spectral power distribution

The spectrum of what arrived, not the spectrum that was emitted.

SPDs are a first-class input — measured, blackbody, white LED, D65 — and per-band RGB grids are accumulated on the receiver. But arrivals do not accumulate a wavelength histogram, so the trace cannot answer "what colour is the light landing here, spectrally", which is the question a filtered, dispersive or phosphor-adjacent system exists to raise.

Partial 0.5 mo Every ray already carries its wavelength. This is one more accumulator on the detector, and it makes the colorimetry above measured rather than assumed.
11

Multi-CPU processing

Use every core on the machine.

Met, and then some. A thread pool over rays with dynamic 512-ray chunks, a SAH BVH, and a four-wide AVX2 leaf intersection probed at run time and bit-for-bit identical to the scalar kernel. Results are bit-identical regardless of thread count — every ray seeds its own RNG from its index and totals reduce in chunk order, asserted in CI on every scene and source combination. The whole 26-scene library at 50 000 rays each traces in 0.51 s.

Exceeds — Determinism is not on LightTools' list. For anyone doing regression work or tolerancing, it is the difference between a reproducible study and a suggestive one.
B

Sources and receivers

10 capabilities

The instruments at each end of the trace. LuxTrace matches LightTools on measured data and on sampling, and falls behind wherever the bullet says user-defined — every distribution it emits is one of a fixed set of laws.

12

Point sources

An emitter of zero extent.

Met. Isotropic point emission is one of three source types, and the app does not quietly substitute a Lambertian emitter to flatter the efficiency figure — most scenes drop to roughly half with a point source, because the backward hemisphere is genuinely lost, and that is reported as the answer.

Parity — Honest by construction. Worth keeping in the pitch.
13

Volume and surface emitters (spheres, cylinders, blocks, toroids)

Light emitted from the whole body of an object, or across its surface, with the emitter shaped like a real part.

Surface emitters: partly met. A source emits over a real emitting area sized by sizeA, sizeB and beamRadius — enough for a rectangular die or a circular aperture, not enough to emit from the surface of an arbitrary solid. Volume emitters: absent. There is no way to say "this whole body glows", which is how a fluorescent tube, a scattering diffuser body or a phosphor volume gets modelled.

Partial 1–1.5 mo Sample the tessellation, not a new primitive. Every object already carries a triangle mesh; area-weighted sampling over it gives surface emission from any solid, and rejection sampling in its bounding volume gives the volume case.
14

User-defined spatial, volume, and angular distributions

Supply your own emission profile as data rather than picking from a menu.

Absent in every axis. Emission is one of three fixed laws — isotropic, Lambertian, or collimated — plus a cone half-angle. There is no I(θ,φ) table, no spatial emission map across the die, no volumetric density. The only escape hatch is a measured ray file, which is a different thing: it supplies rays, not a distribution to sample.

Gap 0.75 mo Cheap and disproportionately useful. A tabulated angular distribution with alias-method sampling also unlocks reading an IES or EULUMDAT file back in as a source — which the app can currently only write.
15

Source emittance aim regions

Concentrate emission toward a nominated region so rays are not wasted on empty space.

The mechanism exists but the control does not. Emission aiming at the scene is part of the estimator work and contributes to error bars 1.5× to 28× tighter at the same ray count — but the target is derived automatically from the scene bounds. A user cannot nominate a region, which is what the bullet describes and what matters when the interesting optic is a small part of a large model.

Partial 0.25 mo Mostly UI over existing machinery — a target volume in the scene document, passed to the aiming code that already exists. One of the cheapest genuine wins here.
16

Spectral distributions: Blackbody, Gaussian, continuous, discrete, and user defined

Five ways to say what colour the source is.

Blackbody, continuous (CIE D65, phosphor-converted white LED), discrete (monochromatic line, three RGB lines) and user-defined (measured SPD) are all implemented. A Gaussian line profile is the only member missing, and it is a few lines against the same sampling interface. One wavelength is sampled per ray, so a spectral trace costs what a monochromatic one costs.

Parity <0.1 mo Add the Gaussian line for laser-diode and filtered-source work, where the linewidth is the point.
17

Angular and spatial importance sampling

Spend rays where they matter instead of uniformly.

Met, and beyond the bullet. Owen-scrambled Sobol emission with replicated scrambling, emission aiming, next-event estimation at diffuse bounces, Russian roulette in place of a hard cutoff, and branch collapsing at interfaces. Elliptical reflector: ±0.312 % → ±0.011 %, a 28× tighter error bar, which is 256× fewer rays for the same answer. Every variance-reduction shortcut books its difference into a residual bucket, so energy still closes to 1e-9.

Exceeds — This is the headline result. An error bar 16× tighter is a hardware-class speedup obtained in the estimator, and it is measured rather than claimed.
18

Ray data sources and Radiant Imaging source model support

Use a vendor's measured LED instead of modelling one.

Met for ray data: Zemax binary .dat, ASAP .dis and TracePro text, dispatched on content rather than extension because vendors ship binary sets as .dat, .ray, .txt and .dis indiscriminately. A capped load takes an evenly strided subset rather than the leading block, so the first N rays of a scanned measurement do not stand in for the whole. Radiant's proprietary source-model format specifically is not read; its exported ray sets are.

Parity — The strided-subset detail is a real correctness point, and most tools get it wrong. Worth saying out loud in any comparison.
19

Surface and far field receivers

Two receiver kinds: one that measures where light lands, one that measures which way it leaves.

Surface: met. Any number, each with its own frame, resolution and acceptance cone; tilted and off-axis receivers bin against themselves. Far field: met but under-resolved. The θ/φ grid totals exactly escaped + detector and exports as IES LM-63 and EULUMDAT — but θ is binned uniformly, so the app's own 1.3° collimated beam falls inside a single 2° bin and the polar cells are starved of samples. The README names equal-solid-angle binning as the fix.

Partial 0.5 mo Fix the binning before showing anyone the collimator. The flagship result is currently under-resolved by the app's own analysis.
20

Angular and spatial luminance meters

Virtual instruments that report cd/m² over angle and over position — the software equivalent of a luminance camera pointed at the part.

Absent. This is the instrument behind item 03's missing quantity, and its absence is why LuxTrace cannot answer glare, display-uniformity or lit-appearance questions at all — the three questions that dominate automotive, display and architectural work.

Gap 1–2 mo · shared with item 03 Buildable forward. A luminance meter is a receiver that bins by position and incoming direction and divides by projected area — it does not require the backward tracer that photorealistic rendering does.
21

Receiver aperture sub-sampling

Sample within each bin rather than accumulating raw hits at bin centres, so the map converges smoothly instead of aliasing.

Absent. Bins accumulate arrivals directly. On a fine grid with a structured source this shows as bin-to-bin noise that is sampling artefact rather than physics — the exact failure mode the low-discrepancy emission sequence was chosen to avoid everywhere else in the pipeline.

Gap 0.5 mo Pairs with bounded receiver memory. Per-thread detector grids already cost 8 MB per thread at 1024², so the accumulation path needs revisiting regardless — do both in one pass.

Where the module boundary falls

Reading the datasheet closely changes two things about the first report, and both are worth carrying forward.

Credited to Core, not Illumination

These are LuxTrace strengths that earn nothing in this report, because LightTools ships them one module lower:

  • Scatter models — LightTools' Core lists "mixed diffuse, narrow angle, and angle of incidence (AOI)"
  • Volume scattering (Mie, user defined) — worth noting that LuxTrace's Henyey–Greenstein is a phase-function model, not one derived from particle size and index. A refinement of the first report's parity call
  • Scattering aim regions, user-defined coatings, probabilistic ray splitting and importance sampling
  • Index of refraction and dispersion formulas
  • Surface patterns of 2D or 3D elements — the texture capability, and it is Core, not Advanced Design

Not held against LuxTrace here

Other modules' work, excluded from this audit by scope:

  • Freeform Design, MacroFocal Reflector, Procedural Rectangle Lens and LED Lens tools — Advanced Design
  • Merit functions, constraints, backlight pattern optimization, parameter sensitivity — Optimization
  • Phosphor particle modeling, GRIN, UDOPs, uniaxial birefringence — Advanced Physics
  • Sequential tracing, paraxial solves, spot diagram and transverse aberration plots — Imaging Path
  • SAT, Parasolid, CATIA V4/V5 import and export — Data Exchange

Months 5–6

The luminance instrument

≈ 2 mo
  • Angular and spatial luminance meters — a receiver binning by position and incoming direction, divided by projected area — item 20
  • Luminance as a reported quantity everywhere illuminance and intensity already appear — item 03
  • Contour and 3D surface charts over the receiver grid — item 04
  • CIE chromaticity diagram with per-bin points, the natural home for colour-over-angle — item 06
The remaining two

Photorealistic rendering and backward ray tracing are one decision, not two. Nine to eighteen months, and it converts LuxTrace from a tool that measures light into one that also shows what the part looks like lit. That is a genuine product pivot, and it should be taken deliberately or not at all — not drifted into because a renderer looks impressive in a screenshot.

If the answer is no, say so in the documentation the way the README already handles wave propagation and thermal coupling: a stated boundary reads as judgement, and an unstated one reads as an omission.
