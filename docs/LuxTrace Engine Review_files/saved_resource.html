02 / Position

Where it stands, honestly

The architecture is better than the physics. Determinism, energy accounting, the shared SurfaceOptics struct, the caching layer, the worker threading, the error bars — those are things commercial tools do and many hobby tracers do not. What is missing is depth in three specific places, and those three are what an evaluator would find in the first hour.

The one-line read

Three items decide whether this competes: exact surface normals (the accuracy ceiling), a real material and BSDF model (the credibility ceiling), and absolute photometric units (the “is this a toy” test). Everything else on this page is refinement on top of those.

03 / Core

Geometry and meshing

The tracer intersects triangles and shades with the triangle’s own flat normal. That single decision sets the accuracy ceiling for every imaging scene in the library, and the code already computes — then discards — exactly what is needed to lift it.

G-1

Interpolate the surface normal instead of using the facet normal

P1Physics

Every triangle carries one flat normal built from a cross product of its edges. A facet normal is off the true surface normal by up to half the angular deflection, and a mirror doubles that into the reflected ray — which is precisely why the elliptical reflector still lands its best focus 22 mm short of the analytic one, and why tightening the mesh from 0.5 rad to 0.06 rad halved the RMS spot. Refining the mesh further buys accuracy at quadratic triangle cost; interpolating the normal buys it for free.

Do this: keep the Poly_Triangulation UV nodes alongside the vertices, and use the barycentric u,v that Möller–Trumbore already returns to interpolate a smooth normal — or, better, evaluate the exact BRepAdaptor_Surface normal at the interpolated UV. intersectTriangle computes u and v and nearestHit throws them away; plumbing them into RayHit is a two-line change that unlocks the largest single accuracy win available.

Shade with the interpolated normal, offset and epsilon-test against the geometric one, and fall back to the geometric normal when the outgoing ray would go back into the surface. This is standard renderer practice and it is what turns a tessellated approximation into a smooth optic.

Expected effect: the tessellation stops being the limiting term, so the mesh can be coarsened for speed while focus quality improves. Both levers move the right way at once.

G-2

Face orientation is ignored, so nothing knows inside from outside

P1PhysicsRobustness

The mesher never checks TopAbs_REVERSED on the face, so triangle winding — and therefore normal direction — is inconsistent across a shape. The tracer papers over it by flipping the normal toward the incident ray on every hit. That works for a single-surface optic and makes correct medium tracking impossible, which is the root of finding M-3.

Do this: flip the winding when the face orientation is reversed, so normals point consistently outward from every solid. Then d·n > 0 genuinely means “leaving” and the tracer can stop guessing.

G-3

Import STEP/IGES and assign optics per face

P1RobustnessInterface

This is the single biggest competitive gap and among the cheapest to close. Illumination tools are bought to be run on a customer’s CAD, not on 25 demo scenes. OCCT already ships the data-exchange toolkits — TKDESTEP, TKDEIGES, TKDESTL are all in the deploy directory — and the CMake target list simply does not link them.

Do this: link the DE toolkits, add a STEP/IGES reader that yields TopoDS_Shapes, and give each imported face an OpticalSurface whose properties are set from a material library in the UI. The whole downstream pipeline — mesh, BVH, trace, analysis — already works on std::vector<OpticalSurface> and needs no change.

The built-in scenes stay valuable as a validation suite and a tutorial library; they stop being the only thing the app can open.

CMakeLists.txt:50–57 — DE toolkits absent from target_link_libraries
G-4

Adaptive tessellation on optically active faces only

P2PerformancePhysics

One deflection setting applies to a whole shape. A parabolic mirror’s reflecting face needs a tight mesh; its rim, its back and the mounting boss do not. The microlens array already needs a hand-tuned coarser hint to avoid half a million triangles.

Do this: set deflection per face from curvature and from whether the face is optically active. Pair it with G-1 and the triangle count for a given spot quality drops by a large factor.

P3Robustness

SceneParams::kMax = 4 is a hard ceiling on how complex any scene can be. It was the right call for a trivially copyable cache key, but a doublet with two radii, two thicknesses, a spacing and a receiver is already six.

Do this: raise to a small fixed-capacity vector (16) that stays trivially copyable and comparable, or key the cache on a hash and keep the parameters in a std::vector<double>.

04 / Core

Materials and surface response

Optical properties are seven loose fields on a struct, filled from four hardcoded constants. This is where the app most visibly reads as a demonstrator rather than an instrument — and where the fix is well-trodden, since every model below has a standard reference implementation.

M-1

A material type with real dispersion models and a glass catalogue

P1Physics

Every glass in the library is n = 1.5 with a single Cauchy B of 0.0042 and one absorption coefficient. Two-term Cauchy is a visible-band fit; it diverges outside it and cannot represent an SF-series flint or a polymer. Nobody specifying an optic works in Cauchy coefficients — they work in catalogue names.

Do this: introduce a Material with a dispersion model — Constant, Cauchy, Sellmeier (3-term), tabulated n(λ), and complex n−ik for metals — plus internal transmittance τ(λ). Ship a small catalogue: N-BK7, N-SF11, fused silica, PMMA, polycarbonate, and Al / Ag / Au. Reference indices at the d line and Abbe numbers come along for free.

Two things fall out immediately. Metal mirrors get complex-index Fresnel instead of a flat 0.95 — an aluminium mirror at 45° is not the same as at normal incidence, and is not the same at 460 nm as at 620. And bulk loss becomes catalogue-derived rather than one number the whole library shares.

SurfaceOptics.h · GeometryProvider.cpp:33–42 kGlassN, kGlassB, kGlassA, kMirrorR
M-2

Replace Gaussian normal jitter with a real BSDF

P1Physics

Roughness is modelled by tilting the normal by tan() of a Gaussian and, when the tilt flips past the incident ray, silently keeping the smooth normal instead. That rejection is a bias toward specular that grows with sigma, and the model has no shadowing–masking term and no normalisation — so it is not an energy-conserving BSDF and it cannot be fitted to measured data.

Do this, in order:

  • Microfacet (GGX or Beckmann) with Smith shadowing–masking and the correct sampling weight, for a polished-but-imperfect surface. Drop-in replacement for the current jitter, same one parameter.
  • ABg / Harvey–Shack scatter — the model both TracePro and LightTools expose, whose A, B, g map directly onto measured BSDF and onto total integrated scatter. This is what lets a user reproduce a scatter spec sheet.
  • Tabulated BSDF import, so measured data can be used as-is.
  • Volume scattering — a scattering coefficient plus a Henyey–Greenstein phase function inside a medium. Absent entirely, and it is among the most-used features in LED luminaire design: every white diffusing plastic in every fixture is a volume scatterer, not a surface one.

Keep pure Lambertian as a special case so the integrating sphere and the matte reflector scenes are unaffected.

Optics.h perturbNormal · RayTracer.cpp:355–360 the rejection branch
M-3

Nested and immersed media — a medium stack, not a single index

P1Physics

The tracer carries one medium integer and treats any transmission while inside something as an exit to vacuum. The comment in the source is candid about the trade. The consequence is that a cemented doublet, a lens in immersion fluid, a clad light guide, an LED die under encapsulant, or a bubble in glass all trace wrongly — and those are ordinary parts, not exotica.

Do this: give each branch a small medium stack (depth 8 is plenty), pushed on entry and popped on exit, so n1 is the top of the stack and n2 is the material of the surface being crossed. For overlapping geometry, add a per-material priority the way FRED and TracePro do, so the higher-priority solid wins where two overlap — this also lets index-matched interfaces (glass→glass) refract correctly instead of always assuming glass→air.

RayTracer.cpp:379 leaving = (s.medium >= 0) · RayTracer.cpp:189 PathState::medium
M-4

Coatings

P1Physics

There are none. Every refractive surface pays bare-glass Fresnel, so a multi-element system overstates loss by roughly 3.5 % per surface against any real lens, which all have coatings. The efficiency figures in the scene table are therefore pessimistic in a way that a user who knows optics will notice at once.

Do this: start with an ideal coating — a specified residual R at normal incidence with the correct angular roll-off — then add an R(λ,θ) table for measured coatings, then a characteristic-matrix thin-film solver for a handful of layers if you want to design them rather than just apply them. The first tier is an afternoon and closes most of the gap.

SurfaceOptics.h · Optics.h fresnelReflectance
M-5

Polarisation via Stokes vectors

P2Physics

Fresnel is the unpolarised average, so Brewster’s angle appears in the reflectance curve but a polariser cannot be modelled, TIR carries no phase, and stress birefringence is out of reach.

Do this: attach a Stokes vector to each branch and apply a Mueller matrix at each interaction. Put it behind a “polarised trace” switch so the unpolarised fast path stays the default — polarised tracing costs roughly 4× the per-ray state and most illumination work does not need it.

This is a genuine lift and it belongs after M-1 through M-4. Ordered here because it is the item that most often appears on a commercial feature matrix.

05 / Core

Sources, spectrum, and units

The source model is well factored — three independent axes, real emitting areas, correct étendue. What is missing is everything downstream of “how much light”: the app called LuxTrace does not currently compute lux.

S-1

Absolute radiometric and photometric units

P1PhysicsInterface

SourceConfig::power exists and defaults to 1.0, and the controls panel has no field for it — so every result is a dimensionless fraction of an unnamed unit. The heatmap axis says “flux per mm²”; the far field says “per steradian”. Neither is a number an engineer can put in a specification.

Do this: add a source flux field with a unit — watts or lumens — and carry it through. Then the irradiance map reads in W/m² or lux, the far field in W/sr or candela, and the encircled-energy curve in real flux. This is a small change with a disproportionate effect on how the tool is perceived.

Photometric output needs S-2 underneath it: lumens are watts weighted by the CIE V(λ) curve, so it requires a spectrum rather than three fixed bands.

RayTracer.h:44 power · ControlsPanel.cpp:218–240 — no power widget
S-2

A real spectrum, not three bands

P1Physics

Spectrum::Rgb splits rays across 620 / 546 / 460 nm and keeps a separate grid per band. A prism therefore produces three beams rather than a spectrum, and there is no way to weight the bands by a source’s actual emission.

Do this: replace the enum with a spectral power distribution — monochromatic, blackbody at a colour temperature, a built-in LED/phosphor SPD, CIE D65, or a user CSV. Sample one wavelength per ray from the SPD (hero-wavelength sampling), which keeps the per-ray cost identical to a monochromatic trace while resolving the spectrum to whatever the ray budget supports.

What this unlocks: lumens and lux via V(λ); CIE xy and CCT of the spot as reportable metrics; and colour-over-angle, the plot LED luminaire designers specifically buy LightTools for. It also makes the RGB heatmap a true colour rendering rather than a three-sample approximation.

S-3

Measured source data and source presets

P2PhysicsInterface

Three analytic angular laws cover the textbook cases and none of the real ones. A real LED is not Lambertian — it has a measured intensity distribution, and manufacturers publish it.

Do this: import an IES LM-63 or ray-file source and sample from it; add named presets (a 1 mm² Lambertian die, a HeNe beam, a fibre with a given NA) so a user picks a source instead of typing four numbers into unlabelled boxes.

06 / Core

The Monte Carlo estimator

This is where accuracy and speed are the same problem. The current estimator is brute force with a hard energy cutoff — correct in structure, biased at the edge, and paying full price for every answer. Fixing it is the highest-leverage performance work in the codebase, and it improves the physics at the same time.

E-1

Russian roulette instead of the hard energy cutoff

P1PhysicsPerformance

Energy below kCut = 1e-4 is discarded and booked into fluxTruncated. That is a systematic bias, not a rounding error: it always removes energy and never adds it, so every efficiency figure is low by the truncated share. It is also why refractive scenes are an order of magnitude slower than reflective ones — every hit spawns both branches, so a path is a tree that only the cutoff prunes.

Do this: terminate low-energy branches by Russian roulette — kill with probability 1−p, scale survivors by 1/p. Unbiased, and it flattens the tree. Then add splitting control: split into both branches only when R and T are both above a threshold; otherwise pick one branch by roulette weighted by its share. On a ball lens that turns a binary tree into a single path for the overwhelming majority of rays.

Keep fluxTruncated as a diagnostic that should read near zero, rather than as a physical loss channel that quietly absorbs the estimator’s error.

Expected effect: refractive scenes speed up several-fold, and the reported efficiency stops being biased low.

RayTracer.cpp:16 kCut · RayTracer.cpp:409–445 both branches always pushed
E-2

Variance reduction: aiming and next-event estimation

P1PerformancePhysics

Nothing steers rays toward the answer. On a Cassegrain at 9.9 % efficiency, ninety percent of the compute produces no signal, and the error bar is set by the rays that miss. Commercial tools solve this with importance sampling, and it is the difference between a ten-second answer and a ten-minute one.

Do this, in order of payoff:

  • Low-discrepancy sampling. Replace independent uniforms with an Owen-scrambled Sobol sequence indexed by ray number. Your counter-based RNG design already gives you the indexing; determinism is preserved exactly. Typical result on smooth integrands: the same error bar from several times fewer rays, for a day of work.
  • Emission aiming. Importance-sample the source solid angle toward the entrance aperture with a compensating weight. Standard in TracePro and LightTools, and the largest win on concentrators and collimators.
  • Next-event estimation at diffuse bounces. Connect each scatter event analytically to the receiver with a visibility test. Order-of-magnitude variance reduction on the integrating sphere and diffuser scenes, where the current tracer is doing a random walk and hoping.
  • Stratify the emitter. Jittered stratification over the emitting area and cone, rather than independent samples.
RayTracer.cpp:72–133 sampleEmission · Optics.h uniform01
E-3

Receiver arrivals are a biased subsample

P1RobustnessPhysics

The arrival budget is divided evenly across chunks, and each chunk keeps the first arrivals it sees until its quota is full. A single trapped ray that arrives fifty times fills its chunk’s quota and censors every later arrival in that chunk. Since through-focus and several spot metrics are computed from arrivals, they are reading a sample skewed toward multi-arrival paths — and the skew is worse on exactly the scenes (light guides, integrating spheres) where paths are long.

Do this: reservoir-sample per chunk, seeded from the chunk index so determinism holds. Every arrival then has equal probability of being kept, regardless of when in the chunk it occurred.

RayTracer.cpp:660–663 chunkArrivalCap · RayTracer.cpp:327 arrivals.size() < arrivalCap
E-4

Numerically fragile reduction and variance

P2Robustness

Two issues in the reduction. The standard error is computed as E[x²] − E[x]², which cancels catastrophically when efficiency is high — on a 90 % scene most of the significant digits are gone before the subtraction. And flux totals accumulate by naive sequential summation over up to 107 rays, so the deterministic answer is deterministic but not maximally accurate.

Do this: Welford’s online algorithm for the variance, and pairwise or Kahan-compensated summation for the flux totals and the grid reduction. Both preserve the chunk-order determinism guarantee exactly.

RayTracer.cpp:774–778 variance · RayTracer.cpp:736–742 grid reduction
E-5

The ray offset epsilon is absolute, not scale-relative

P2Robustness

kEps = 1e-6 is used both as the minimum hit distance and as the fixed offset when spawning a branch (p + dir * kEps). In a scene measured in millimetres near the origin that is fine; at a metre from the origin, double precision has about 1e−13 relative resolution, so a grazing hit can self-intersect or leak through. It also fails in the other direction: a microlens feature smaller than a few microns gets over-offset.

Do this: offset along the geometric normal by a magnitude proportional to the hit point’s coordinate magnitude, and set tMin per-ray from the same scale. The standard robust-offset formulation costs a handful of integer operations and eliminates a whole class of “why is there a hole in my optic” reports.

E-6

Track optical path length

P2Physics

A branch carries position, direction, energy, medium and depth — but not accumulated OPL. Adding one double per branch is nearly free and unlocks a disproportionate amount: optical path difference maps, a wavefront error estimate, a Strehl-style figure, a diffraction-limit reference to plot against the geometric spot, and time-of-flight for pulsed work.

Do this: accumulate n × distance per leg into PathState and record it on each DetectorArrival. The analysis layer already has everything else it needs.

E-7

The non-Fresnel TIR path pushes two branches into the same half-space

P3Physics

When Fresnel is switched off and a ray hits TIR, the transmitted energy is pushed along the reflected direction — and the reflected branch is pushed separately along the same direction. Energy is conserved, but the surface produces two coincident reflected rays with a split that has no physical meaning. It only bites in the “Fresnel off” comparison mode, which is why it has not shown up.

Do this: at TIR with Fresnel off, merge the two into one branch carrying reflE + transE.

07 / Measurement

Detectors and the far field

D-1

One receiver, planar, axis-aligned, 64×64, hardcoded

P1RobustnessInterface

Four restrictions compound here. The receiver must face +Z. Only the first detector surface in a scene becomes the receiver — a second one still terminates rays and books their flux, but bins them against the first detector’s frame, which is a latent correctness bug waiting for the first two-detector scene. And the grid is detNX = detNY = 64, written into the mesher, unreachable from the UI.

Do this: store a full receiver frame (origin plus two tangent vectors) instead of assuming +Z; support a list of named detectors each with its own frame, grid and units; expose resolution in the UI. Add an acceptance-cone filter per detector so a ray arriving outside the cone is not counted — that is how a real photometer and a real fibre behave.

Then add the detector types the analysis already almost supports: intensity detectors (angular bins on a surface) and radiance detectors (position × angle), which is what étendue-limited design actually needs.

MeshBuilder.cpp:74–86 hardcoded 64×64, +Z assumption · TraceScene.cpp:84 !m_det.valid takes only the first
D-2

Far-field bins are uniform in θ, so the on-axis peak is one bin wide

P1Physics

90 θ bins over 180° is a 2° step. The parabolic reflector’s headline result is a 1.3° beam FWHM — resolved by less than one bin, and reported through an interpolated crossing between two of them. Meanwhile the cell solid angle varies by four orders of magnitude between the equator and the pole, so the polar cells are starved of samples and noisy exactly where a collimator’s answer lives.

Do this: bin on equal solid angle (a sinusoidal or HEALPix-style scheme) so every cell collects comparable statistics, and auto-refine the on-axis region when the distribution is peaked. At minimum, make the resolution adaptive to the measured FWHM rather than fixed at 90×72.

RayTracer.cpp:216–229 binDirection · RayTracer.cpp:456–470 finishIntensity
D-3

Export IES LM-63 and EULUMDAT

P1InterfacePhysics

The far-field grid is already the candela distribution a luminaire is specified by — it is one unit conversion and one file writer away from being the file format the entire lighting industry consumes. Nothing else on this list gives a comparable ratio of usefulness to effort.

Do this: once S-1 gives absolute units, write the θ/φ table as IES LM-63 and as EULUMDAT. A user can then take a LuxTrace result straight into DIALux, AGi32 or Relux. That is a genuine interoperability claim, and the sort of thing that gets a tool adopted.

08 / Measurement

Analysis and parameter studies

The analysis layer is the strongest part of the app — densities rather than bin counts, error bars on everything, a focus sweep as a post-process. The gap is not quality, it is coverage: two studies exist where the infrastructure supports five.

A-1

Sweep the geometry parameters — the study the app is already built for

P1Interface

Every scene declares its parameters with ranges, steps and units. There is a shared-ownership geometry cache designed for exactly the case of sweeping through parameter values. There is a study worker with progress and cancellation. And the only sweeps offered are ray count and focus position.

Do this: a parameter sweep — efficiency, RMS radius, uniformity or FWHM against any one of the scene’s own dimensions, plotted with error bars on the existing PlotWidget. Then a 2D sweep over a parameter pair as a contour map. This is a small amount of work on top of what already exists, and it turns the app from a viewer into a design tool.

Studies.h · Simulation.cpp:19 kVariantCacheSize — already built for this
A-2

Optimisation against a merit function

P1Interface

The step after A-1, and the reason people pay for OpticStudio. Given a parameter vector and a merit function — maximise efficiency, minimise RMS radius, hit a target uniformity — search for the best design.

Do this: Nelder–Mead first (derivative-free, handles the Monte Carlo noise, and short), then CMA-ES for the harder landscapes. Feed it the deterministic tracer with a fixed seed so the objective is a genuine function rather than a noisy one — your determinism guarantee is what makes this work cleanly, and it is a real advantage over tools whose objective jitters between evaluations.

A-3

Tolerance analysis

P2PhysicsInterface

The question a manufacturer actually asks is not “how good is the nominal design” but “what fraction of production will pass”. Nothing here answers it.

Do this: perturb parameters, decentres, tilts and surface figure by specified distributions; trace an ensemble; report a yield histogram against a pass criterion, and a sensitivity ranking showing which tolerance dominates. The determinism story makes the ensemble reproducible, which tolerancing tools rarely are.

A-4

Image-quality metrics beyond the spot

P2Physics

The spot metrics are well chosen and correctly computed as densities. What is missing is the frequency-domain half: MTF from the point-spread function the arrivals already describe, and — once E-6 tracks optical path length — an OPD map and a diffraction-limit reference to plot the geometric spot against.

Do this: LSF from the arrival distribution, FFT to MTF, plot beside the encircled-energy curve. Add the Airy radius as a reference marker so a user can see when the design has stopped being geometry-limited.

A-5

Through-focus should refuse rather than mislead

P3Robustness

The sweep propagates arrivals in a straight line, which is exact only where nothing stands between the planes. The limit is documented but not enforced — sweep across an intervening optic and the result is confidently wrong.

Do this: test the swept span against the scene bounds and grey out or warn on the range where geometry intervenes. A tool that says “I cannot answer that here” is worth more than one that answers anyway.

09 / Speed

Performance

The big win is already banked — 48 s to 0.17 s. The remaining headroom is substantial before any GPU work, and most of it comes from the estimator (E-1, E-2) rather than from the intersection kernel. These are the structural items underneath that.

P-1

Float bounds and a wide BVH

P2Performance

A BvhNode holds six doubles of bounds plus four ints — 64 bytes, one full cache line per node, and the slab test is the hot loop. Traversal is a binary walk with a scalar three-axis slab test.

Do this: store bounds as floats rounded outward (conservative, so no hit is lost) while keeping the triangle intersection in double — halves node size immediately. Then go to a 4- or 8-wide BVH with a SIMD slab test against all children at once.

TraceScene.h:68–76 BvhNode · TraceScene.cpp:255–305 traversal
P-2

Two-level BVH for repeated geometry

P2Performance

The 5×5 microlens array tessellates twenty-five copies of one lenslet and builds one BVH over all of them — which is why it needs a hand-tuned coarse mesh hint to stay under a three-second build.

Do this: a top-level acceleration structure over instances, each referencing one shared bottom-level BVH with a transform. Twenty-five lenslets become one mesh, one BVH, twenty-five transforms. Build time and memory collapse, and the mesh can be made finer rather than coarser. This also generalises to any imported CAD assembly with repeated parts.

TraceScene.cpp:56–104 build · GeometryProvider.h:26 per-shape mesh hint
P-3

Parallel BVH build

P3Performance

buildNode recurses on one thread. It is not the bottleneck for a single run, but it is what a user feels when dragging a parameter slider, since every step mints new geometry that misses the cache.

Do this: parallelise the top few levels of the recursion as tasks. Cheap, and it directly improves the interactive feel of the thing users touch most.

TraceScene.cpp:120 buildNode
P-4

Per-thread detector grids will not scale past 64×64

P2PerformanceRobustness

Each thread gets a full copy of the irradiance grid, tripled on a spectral run. At 64×64 that is 32 KB per thread and entirely reasonable. At the 1024×1024 resolution D-1 makes reachable, it is 8 MB per thread — 256 MB on a 32-thread machine, tripled again for spectral, and the final reduction becomes a serial pass over a quarter-gigabyte.

Do this: tile the detector and give each thread sparse tiles allocated on first touch, or accumulate into a shared grid with per-thread write coalescing. Keep the deterministic reduction order either way.

R-1

R-2

Validate against closed-form optics, not only against recomputed arithmetic

P1PhysicsRobustness

108 tests is real coverage, and pinning Fresnel against Rs and Rp recomputed in the test is the right instinct. But recomputing the same formula proves the implementation matches itself. What convinces an engineer evaluating the tool is agreement with independent analytic optics.

Add a --validate mode that checks the tracer against results derived outside it:

  • Paraxial focal length of the plano-convex and biconvex lenses against the lensmaker’s equation.
  • Integrating-sphere throughput against the sphere multiplier M = ρ / (1 − ρ(1 − f)).
  • Irradiance from a point source on a tilted plane against the inverse-square cosine law.
  • CPC acceptance angle against sin θmax = 1/C for the concentration ratio built.
  • Prism deviation against the minimum-deviation formula, per band.
  • A Lambertian disc’s far field against I(θ) = I0 cos θ.

Publish the table with residuals. That page is a better sales argument than any feature list, and every one of these has a textbook closed form.

12 / Sequence

What to do, in what order

Ordered so each phase makes the next one cheaper. The dependencies are real: units need a spectrum, coatings need materials, and nearly everything downstream is easier once normals are correct.

Phase 1 — Lift the accuracy ceiling

The core, first · G-1, G-2, M-3, E-1, E-3

Interpolated surface normals and consistent face orientation together remove the tessellation limit that currently caps every imaging scene. Medium stacking makes multi-element and immersed optics tractable. Russian roulette removes the truncation bias and pays for itself in speed on refractive scenes. Reservoir-sampled arrivals fix a bias that quietly affects the focus study.

Nothing in this phase changes an API the UI touches, and each item is independently testable. Re-baseline the whole scene library afterwards — the numbers will move, and that movement is the deliverable.

Phase 2 — Become measurable

Credibility · M-1, S-1, S-2, D-1, D-3, R-2

A material type with Sellmeier and a glass catalogue; a source flux in watts or lumens; a real spectrum sampled per ray. Together these turn every output from a fraction into a physical quantity, which is the difference between a demonstrator and an instrument. Then arbitrary detector frames and resolution, IES export, and a validation suite against closed-form optics with the residuals published.

Phase 3 — Become fast enough to iterate

Speed & feel · E-2, P-5, P-1, P-2

Sobol sampling and emission aiming cut the rays needed for a given error bar by a large factor. Progressive results change how the app feels more than any kernel optimisation will. Float BVH bounds, a wide traversal and instancing then take the kernel itself further.

Phase 4 — Become a design tool

Workflow · A-1, A-2, U-1, U-2, U-3, U-4, G-3

Parameter sweeps and optimisation against a merit function; derived quantities beside the parameters; run comparison; a one-click report; and STEP import so the tool can be pointed at work that already exists. This phase is where the app stops answering “what does this do” and starts answering “what should I build”.

Phase 5 — Depth

Specialisation · M-2, M-4, M-5, A-3, A-4

The full BSDF stack with ABg scatter and volume scattering; coatings; polarisation via Stokes vectors; tolerance analysis; MTF and OPD. These are the items that appear on a commercial feature matrix, and each is worth doing properly once the foundation under it is sound.

If only three things get done

G-1 interpolated normals, because it lifts the accuracy ceiling for free from data the code already computes and discards. S-1 with S-2, because a tool that reports fractions instead of lux will not be taken seriously by the people it is for. E-1 Russian roulette, because it removes a real bias and makes the slowest scenes several times faster in the same change.
